Does Hydrogen Water Work? Two Real-World European Market Cases and Their Full Dossiers

Published: Oct 17, 2026 • Author: European Molecular Hydrogen Research Observatory • Category: Sports Physiology & Evidence-Based Science • Read Time: ~10 min

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Two Real-World Commercial Cases: UK brand HtoEAU (NCT06788015) and Czech brand Nutristamina (NCT05799911) both completed rigorous randomized, double-blind, crossover clinical trials via independent university faculties.
  • Divergent Attitudes Toward Transparency: The UK brand claimed universal improvements on its retail site but withheld trial results on ClinicalTrials.gov with zero peer-reviewed papers. The Czech study documented a +0.6% shift in 400 m finswimming alongside four null endpoints, published two journal papers, and deposited open raw data on Zenodo.
  • Nuanced Statistical Reality: The +0.6% shift was 1.5 times the smallest worthwhile change (SWC = 0.4%), crossing athletic relevance, yet Cohen's d was -0.07 (trivial). Statistical significance coexisted with minute physical effect, and blood lactate remained unchanged.
  • True Mark of Scientific Maturity: An empirical field matures not when every actor claims success, but when researchers openly document null outcomes and methodological boundaries.

In the European marketplace, two full-cycle experiments on hydrogen water have already been conducted on our behalf.

These are not theoretical hypotheses in academic journals, nor are they lab bench concepts.

Two real commercial enterprises formulated physical products, assigned prices, placed them onto retail shelves, and sold them to consumers. Both subsequently funded university research teams to conduct clinical trials—and then either published their complete results, or chose not to.

One brand is based in the United Kingdom, priced at £2.49 per can, with its homepage prominently displaying "Clinically tested. Measurably effective."

The other is based in the Czech Republic, tested on national-level finswimmers in a double-blind crossover trial, concluding that 400-meter swimming times changed by +0.6%.

The clinical designs of the two trials were virtually identical: randomized, double-blind, crossover, and placebo-controlled.

Their reported conclusions diverge substantially.

Their attitudes toward scientific transparency diverge even further.

We extracted the complete dossiers for both cases—including corporate entities, product formulations, clinical registry IDs, peer-reviewed papers, and raw open datasets. Below is the side-by-side comparative analysis of these two European journeys.

1. Clarifying Criteria: What Constitutes a "Real-World Case"?

In this investigation, a "real-world case" must satisfy four non-negotiable criteria:

First, the product must be actively commercialized.
It must possess a verifiable retail price, commercial packaging, and active sales channels—not a concept or an internal lab prototype.

Second, the corporate entity must be legally established.
It must have a registered corporate name, manufacturing origin, and legal identity—not vague assertions such as "developed by a specialist research team."

Third, the clinical study must have been genuinely executed.
It must carry a clinical registry number (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov NCT ID), enrolled human subjects, and formal institutional ethics committee approval.

Fourth, the outcomes must be publicly verifiable.
The findings must either be openly accessible or demonstrably unpublished—because withholding data is itself informative.

Only when all four criteria are met does an investigation qualify as a case study. Without them, it remains mere marketing narrative.

Why do real-world cases carry greater weight than selective literature reviews?

Because literature can be cited selectively; real-world cases cannot.

In an academic review, one can easily cite a single positive finding while omitting null endpoints. But when a physical product sits on a store shelf, capital has been deployed, human subjects have completed protocols, and dataset matrices have been locked—the resulting reality must be confronted.

2. Case A: The £2.49 Canned Hydrogen Water from the UK

The Enterprise and Product.

HtoEAU is a canned hydrogen water brand marketed with UK pricing. Its marketing materials cite Icelandic glacial water, infused with 5 mg/L (5 ppm) dissolved molecular hydrogen, a pH of 8.3, and formulated without added sugars, stimulants, or preservatives. The manufacturing process claims three-stage filtration, ultraviolet sterilization, and pressurized canning to retain dissolved gas. It retails from £2.49 per can.

📸 Real Commercial Product & Brand Dossier · Case A (UK HtoEAU)
Verifiable Product on Shelf: UK commercial HtoEAU canned hydrogen water (labeled 5 mg/L, retail £2.49/can, approx. 330 mL/can).
Brand Identity: Official HtoEAU brand insignia, positioned in the UK premium functional beverage sector citing Icelandic glacial water.

The Principal Investigator.

The study was led by Professor Sergej Ostojić of the University of Novi Sad, Serbia. Professor Ostojić is an established figure in molecular hydrogen research, with over 320 peer-reviewed articles, an H-index of 41, and 11 patents. The brand's website highlights him as "the first scientist outside Asia to publish clinical research on hydrogen water."

🎓 Investigator & Academic Institution Dossier · Case A (University of Novi Sad)
Principal Investigator: Prof. Sergej M. Ostojić, MD, PhD (University of Novi Sad; MHI Advisory Panel member; 320+ publications, H-index 41).
Executing Institution: University of Novi Sad Central Campus (Serbia), whose FSPE Applied Bioenergetics Lab executed the protocol.

The Study Protocol.

The trial spanned 4 weeks and employed a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover design. Participants were collegiate athletes with over three years of structured training and more than five hours of weekly athletic conditioning.

Double-blinding ensured that neither participants nor investigators knew whether active hydrogen water or placebo was administered—both beverages were identical in appearance, taste, and packaging. Crossover assignment meant that after an intervening period, participants swapped groups: those initially consuming active water switched to placebo, and vice versa. The crossover model provides robust statistical power by allowing each participant to act as their own control, canceling out inter-individual variance and enabling lean sample sizes.

According to the official ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT06788015), the actual enrollment was 10 registered (12 claimed on website), representing 20 participant-periods.

It was an elegantly structured protocol.

The outcome reported by the brand: four physiological and wellbeing endpoints significantly improved (brand self-report).

3. The Four Endpoints Translated into Plain English

The four endpoints highlighted on the brand's website translate directly into everyday athletic and life terms:

1. Maximum Running Velocity (vmax):
The absolute top sprinting speed achievable during an incremental treadmill test—analogous to an all-out sprint to catch a closing subway door.

2. Anaerobic Threshold Velocity (vANT):
While technical in terminology, this metric has the most direct bearing on daily physical stamina. It represents the critical velocity at which the body transitions from sustainable aerobic metabolism to rapid blood lactate accumulation. In everyday language: the maximum pace you can sustain continuously without feeling breathless.

Sustaining a brisk uphill walk, climbing five flights of stairs without gasping, or swimming continuously for 20 minutes all depend on this threshold. In endurance sports, it is among the most prized indicators because it dictates not merely how fast you can burst, but how long you can maintain speed.

3. Mental Health Assessment Score:
Standardized psychometric evaluation measuring subjective mood stability, stress resilience, and psychological wellbeing.

4. SF-36 General Health Survey:
The internationally recognized 36-item Short Form Health Survey evaluating eight functional health domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, bodily pain, general health perceptions, vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health.

This four-parameter matrix balances two objective physical capacity metrics with two subjective wellbeing assessments.

4. Case B: The Czech National Finswimming Cohort

A Protocol Under Rigorous Laboratory Controls.

14 athletes were initially recruited, with 12 completing the full trial (8 female, 4 male). All participants were competitive national-level finswimmers. Participants were aged 16 to 30, non-smokers, engaged in systematic athletic training. Strict compliance protocols prohibited any dietary supplements for 3 weeks prior to testing, and strictly barred strenuous exertion, alcohol, and caffeine for 24 hours prior to trials.

The Investigational Product.

The test beverage was Aquastamina-R, produced by Nutristamina s.r.o. based in Ostrava, Czech Republic. The dissolved hydrogen concentration was certified at 0.9 ppm, packaged in 420 mL sealed aluminum-plastic pouches. The placebo consisted of degassed water from the identical production batch—packaged identically and distinguishable only by internal batch codes.

📸 Real Commercial Product & Enterprise Dossier · Case B (Czech Nutristamina)
Verifiable Product on Shelf: Aquastamina-R 420 mL aluminum-plastic pouch (tested at 0.9 ppm dissolved H₂, commercialized by Nutristamina).
Manufacturing Enterprise: Nutristamina s.r.o. (based in Ostrava, Czech Republic, specializing in molecular hydrogen hydration & sports nutrition).

Crucially, the manufacturer kept the coding confidential until the entire study was completed.

Because even the product sponsor remained blinded during data collection, the study adhered to strict double-blind standards.

🎓 Research Team & Laboratory Dossier · Case B (Palacký University Olomouc)
Corresponding Author: Prof. PhDr. Michal Botek, Ph.D. (Faculty of Physical Culture, Palacký University Olomouc, specializing in exercise physiology & H₂).
Active Research Facility: The molecular hydrogen research team at FTK UPOL conducting exercise physiology and performance diagnostic testing.
Executing University: Palacký University Olomouc (Czech Republic), established in 1573 as one of Central Europe's leading public research universities.

The Verified Findings:

  • 400-meter finswimming time: +0.6% improvement (95% CI 0.2% to 0.9%), statistically significant (P = 0.003)
  • 50-meter sprint time: No significant difference (+0.5%, P = 0.12)
  • Blood lactate concentration: No significant difference (P ≥ 0.12)
  • Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE): No significant difference (P ≥ 0.12)
  • Plasma protein carbonyls (oxidative stress marker): No significant difference (P ≥ 0.12)

Among the 12 athletes who completed the full trial, 8 demonstrated a positive response, while 4 showed zero response.

The authors candidly detailed two key methodological limitations: performance was timed using manual stopwatches rather than electronic touchpads, and round-the-clock dietary and sleep monitoring was unfeasible in free-living conditions.

The authors made no sweeping generalizations regarding overall product efficacy. Instead, they reported a +0.6% change in a specific 400 m event, alongside four null physiological endpoints and a one-third non-responder rate.

The distinction is not about who reported larger gains, but who made their data subject to independent verification.

A further discrepancy is worth noting: Case A's website highlights "maximum velocity" and "anaerobic threshold velocity," yet neither metric appears in the official registered outcome list on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06788015). In scientific communication, silence regarding discrepancies is informative.

6. Why the +0.6% Study Carries Greater Scientific Weight

The credibility of the Czech study stems not from the modest size of the number, but from its willingness to document where no effect occurred.

A detailed examination reveals five hallmarks of scientific rigor:

1. Independent Peer Review:
The primary trial was published in the International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance (IJSPP, July 2026). A complementary trial on muscle recovery from the same cohort was published in Frontiers in Physiology (2024).

2. Full Disclosure of Null Findings:
Out of five core metrics evaluated, four showed no statistically significant differences. In commercially supplied research, documenting null findings requires integrity.

3. Open-Access Raw Data:
The complete anonymized dataset was deposited on Zenodo, allowing any independent researcher to audit, re-analyze, and verify the statistical calculations.

4. Explicit Methodological Limitations:
The authors openly disclosed that manual hand-timing was utilized and that continuous nutritional and sleep monitoring was unfeasible.

5. Documentation of Individual Variance:
The paper recorded 8 responders and 4 non-responders, refusing to mask biological heterogeneity behind a generic claim of universal efficacy.

Together, these five factors constitute verifiable evidence.

In contrast, Case A reported four improved endpoints (brand self-report), yet no results have been submitted to ClinicalTrials.gov, and no corresponding peer-reviewed paper is discoverable in PubMed.

While this does not prove wrongdoing, it establishes that its claims cannot be independently verified.

7. Is +0.6% Truly Meaningful?

Regarding the +0.6% observed in Case B, the authors provided two distinct statistical benchmarks:

Benchmark 1: Smallest Worthwhile Change (SWC).

In elite sports science, SWC defines the threshold where a performance shift is large enough to exceed routine competitive variance and carry practical sporting significance. The authors set the SWC for 400 m finswimming at 0.4% (one-half the typical between-competition variation). The observed improvement of 0.6% (95% CI 0.2% to 0.9%) was 1.5 times the SWC, crossing the benchmark for athletic relevance.

Benchmark 2: Standardized Effect Size (Cohen's d).

The authors also calculated the standardized effect size Cohen's d to quantify the magnitude of difference relative to overall variance. The calculation yielded d = −0.07, classified by the authors' established criteria as "trivial."

This highlights a common phenomenon in contemporary sports science: statistical significance coexisting with trivial effect magnitude.

Both findings are valid and mathematically non-contradictory:

• Statistical Significance (P = 0.003) indicates that under strict crossover controls—where each subject serves as their own baseline—the probability of this 0.6% shift arising purely from random sampling error is very low.

• Trivial Effect Size (d = −0.07) indicates that the absolute physical scale of the shift remains minute relative to overall population variance.

The authors described the outcome as "modest yet meaningful," capturing the nuanced scientific reality.

Crucially, blood lactate levels remained statistically unchanged. If the mechanism of dissolved hydrogen were simply accelerating lactate clearance as frequently asserted in popular discourse, post-exercise lactate should have dropped. Because lactate remained unchanged while performance shifted slightly, the physiological pathway likely operates elsewhere—such as hypothesized mitochondrial bioenergetic efficiency. This hypothesis remains unconfirmed, but it establishes a precise, falsifiable research question for future trials.

8. What These Two Cases Teach Us

First, an overlooked physical parameter: Case B utilized water with a dissolved hydrogen concentration of only 0.9 ppm.

Case A prominently advertised 5 mg/L (5 ppm). If concentration alone dictated biological response, 0.9 ppm should have yielded zero measurable signals. Yet the Czech trial captured a statistically significant shift at 0.9 ppm.

This indicates that concentration is not the sole determinant.

Administration timing, athlete training status, metabolic demands, and endpoint sensitivity all influence outcomes. Isolated marketing fixations on high ppm numbers disregard the physiological reality of delivery protocols.

From a broader disciplinary perspective: Modern biomedical hydrogen research traces its foundation to Professor Shigeo Ohta's landmark 2007 paper in Nature Medicine, demonstrating selective scavenging of toxic hydroxyl radicals. In the 18 years since, European academic centers have established rigorous infrastructure: registered trials, crossover double-blind designs, peer-reviewed journals, open datasets, and documented null endpoints in elite athletes. This is what an evidence-based discipline looks like as it matures.

A scientific field proves its maturity not when every study proclaims success, but when researchers openly publish null outcomes and methodological boundaries.

The Czech team's study found no differences across four out of five parameters, yet was published in a top-tier physiology journal. That publication carries vastly greater weight than unverified commercial claims.

Only concepts that withstand the possibility of falsification belong in science.

Hydrogen water today is not a finalized commercial conclusion; it is an active, rigorous subject of ongoing physiological inquiry.

9. Four Questions to Ask When Evaluating "Clinically Tested" Claims

When encountering commercial products claiming clinical validation, four questions cut through the noise:

1. Is there an active clinical trial registry ID?
A verifiable NCT number on ClinicalTrials.gov confirms that trial design, sample size, and endpoints were pre-registered, preventing post-hoc narrative shifts.

2. Who sponsored and executed the study?
Trials sponsored by independent universities carry higher methodological insulation than internal corporate testing.

3. What was the actual sample size?
Single-digit cohorts are exploratory; dozens provide preliminary indications; hundreds are required for definitive therapeutic conclusions. A 10-person study can only detect exploratory signals, not establish broad efficacy.

4. Are the full results and raw data publicly accessible?
Unpublished, unreviewed internal whitepapers do not constitute clinical evidence in evidence-based medicine.

These four questions require no specialized background and cut directly through informational fog.

10. Concluding Perspective

The global wellness market abounds with conceptual claims and marketing narratives.

Yet cases that furnish a public clinical registry number, verifiable open data, and peer-reviewed journal papers remain rare.

The Czech academic team delivered all three: pre-registration, peer review, and open-access raw data—faithfully documenting a modest +0.6% shift alongside four null physiological endpoints.

The British brand designed a structured crossover trial, but withheld its full dataset from public registries while featuring "clinically tested" claims on its retail storefront.

What healthy industry development requires is not louder promotional narratives, but verifiable scientific evidence.

And evaluating such claims does not require a degree in physiology.

It merely requires asking: Where can I independently review the original dossier?

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q: What were the verified findings of the Czech finswimming double-blind trial?

In the randomized, double-blind, crossover trial conducted by Palacký University Olomouc (NCT05799911), 0.9 ppm hydrogen-rich water supplementation resulted in a statistically significant +0.6% improvement in 400 m finswimming time (P=0.003), crossing the smallest worthwhile change (SWC=0.4%) threshold. However, Cohen's d effect size was -0.07 (trivial), and four other endpoints (50 m sprint, blood lactate, RPE, and plasma protein carbonyls) showed zero significant difference (P>=0.12), with 4 out of 12 athletes exhibiting zero response. The complete dataset is openly hosted on Zenodo.

Q: How does UK Case A (NCT06788015) compare with Czech Case B in scientific transparency?

Both studies utilized high-standard randomized double-blind crossover protocols. The critical divergence lies in disclosure: UK brand HtoEAU claimed on its website that four athletic and wellbeing metrics improved significantly, yet no outcome data were posted to ClinicalTrials.gov and no peer-reviewed paper exists. In contrast, the Czech study published two peer-reviewed journal papers (IJSPP and Frontiers in Physiology) fully detailing null findings alongside the 0.6% shift, and deposited raw anonymized data on Zenodo.

Q: Why is +0.6% characterized as 'modest yet meaningful' in sports physiology?

The study utilized two distinct benchmarks: in competitive sports science, the smallest worthwhile change (SWC) of 0.4% represents practical significance, and 0.6% is 1.5 times the SWC. However, the standardized effect size Cohen's d was -0.07, categorized as trivial relative to overall cohort variation. Statistical significance (P=0.003) confirmed that the shift was not random error under crossover controls, yet the absolute physical effect was minute and blood lactate remained unchanged.
